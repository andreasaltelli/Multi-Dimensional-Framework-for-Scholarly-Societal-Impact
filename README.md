The evaluation of scientific impact is increasingly expected to extend beyond citation
counts to encompass policy influence, public visibility, technological innovation,
and cross-disciplinary reach. Yet citation based scores have rarely been empirically
tested against such extra-bibliometric dimensions. This paper presents a pilot study
comparing the predictive validity of the h-index and the Stanford/Elsevier Science-
Wide Author Database composite score across multiple societal impact metrics for
250 scientists each from Economics & Business and Clinical Medicine. Using an automated
multi-source pipeline integrating Scopus, Overton, EPO, Wikipedia, NIH
iCite, and other data sources, we compute Spearman rank correlations and paired
bootstrap confidence intervals for all outcome dimensions per field. Results are
field-contingent: the Stanford composite substantially outperforms the h-index in
Economics & Business across quality-weighted citations, policy document mentions,
patent counts, and public prominence, while the h-index retains stronger predictive
validity in Clinical Medicine, where volume-quality correlation is high and large
collaborative citation networks dominate. Across both fields and both metrics, correlations
with the Rao-Stirling Diversity index — a measure of cross-disciplinary
breadth — are consistently near zero, suggesting that interdisciplinary scientists remain
structurally invisible to citation-based evaluation regardless of methodological
sophistication. We argue that this is not a correctable design flaw but an architectural
feature of citation graphs. The pipeline developed here offers a reproducible
methodology for augmenting bibliometric records with societal impact data at scale.
